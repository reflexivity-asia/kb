# Trace hawkish-policy beneficiaries from macro channels to companies

**Author:** Reflexivity Research  
**Primary asset classes:** Equities, fixed income, FX, cross-asset  
**Intended users:** Macro PMs, multi-asset PMs, equity PMs  
**Analysis type:** Knowledge graph, thematic analysis, investment-universe construction

> This page preserves the structure and limitations of an actual Reflexivity research output. The point is not to claim that every graph link implies direct earnings sensitivity, but to show how a macro view can be translated into a research universe.

## Research question

Instead of stopping at “hawkish policy means higher rates,” the research traced the idea through three levels:

1. **First-order macro channels**
2. **Second-order sectors**
3. **Third-order named companies**

The goal was to use the Knowledge Graph to turn a policy thesis into a concrete list of assets, sectors, and companies worth investigating next.

## First-order effects: transmission channels

The source grouped the main beneficiary channels into three themes:

1. **Higher-for-longer yields**
2. **Energy / inflation**
3. **Geopolitical spending**

## Second-order effects: sectors

The research then mapped those channels into sectors:

- Higher-for-longer → banks and diversified financials, insurers, asset and money managers
- Energy / inflation → energy producers
- Geopolitical spending → aerospace and defense

The rates channel produced the broadest beneficiary set because higher yields can affect bank net interest margins, insurer reinvestment yields, money-market economics, and cash yields.

## Third-order effects: companies

Examples of high-ranking Knowledge Graph constituents in the source included:

- **Financials:** CBOE, Charles Schwab, Morgan Stanley
- **Insurers:** Chubb, Progressive, Swiss Re
- **Asset managers:** BlackRock, Franklin Resources, Invesco
- **Energy:** ConocoPhillips, SLB, Saudi Arabian Oil
- **Defense:** Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman, RTX

The source also surfaced candidates across consumer lending, sustainable utilities, commercial real estate, homebuilding, automakers, credit and lending, and other related areas.

## How to read the graph

Graph or Sankey link width is **not** a direct measure of cash flow, earnings sensitivity, or expected return.

In the source it functions as an explanatory proxy:

- Root → channel: how broadly that channel connects into sectors
- Sector → company: Knowledge Graph exposure rank

That distinction matters because graph prominence can identify where to investigate next without proving that a company will benefit economically.

## Analytical caveats

- The first-/second-/third-order hierarchy is an analyst framing; the Knowledge Graph itself does not certify causality.
- Graph links do not measure company-level profit sensitivity directly.
- Higher-for-longer rates may support financial-sector margins while also worsening credit quality or loan demand.
- Actual sensitivity differs substantially across companies within the same sector.

The use case therefore ends with a **research universe**, not a buy list. Company fundamentals, balance-sheet exposure, valuation, and scenario sensitivity still need to be tested.

## What this use case demonstrates

This workflow shows how to translate a macro policy view into a structured chain of transmission channels, sectors, and named companies, then hand those candidates into deeper fundamental research.
