# Compare oil-price surges by regime and trace the sector impact

**Author:** QUICK Corporation  
**Provided:** 2026-09-14  
**Primary asset classes:** Commodities, equities, macro, multi-asset

> This page preserves a use case provided by QUICK while removing customer, recipient, signature, and private-link information. Market levels and interpretations are a dated snapshot from the source material, not a current forecast.

## Original question

How did the drivers of the oil-price rise after the March US-Iran conflict differ from the rise since August, and which industries and major companies were affected by higher oil prices?

## What the research is trying to establish

Two oil rallies can look similar on a price chart while being economically very different.

If the driver is a **physical supply disruption**, the persistence, inflation transmission, and sector impact can differ from a rally driven by **demand expectations, production policy, or geopolitical risk premia**.

The research therefore follows this sequence:

1. separate the March and post-August periods into distinct regimes;
2. identify what the source material considered the dominant oil-price driver in each regime;
3. compare how the same oil-price shock transmitted into energy, airlines, and chemicals;
4. identify the variables that would sustain or reverse the current regime.

That produces a chain of **oil move → cause of the move → cost/revenue transmission → sector and company reaction**.

## Two different oil-price regimes

### March episode — geopolitical supply shock

The QUICK-provided source characterized the March episode as a sudden geopolitical supply shock tied to the US-Iran war and severe disruption risk around Gulf/Hormuz exports.

In the source snapshot:

- WTI moved from roughly **$62.89 in mid-February to $112.95 in early April**;
- the main interpretation was physical-supply disruption risk rather than a gradual demand repricing;
- the episode was treated as an abrupt shock whose persistence depended heavily on the actual supply route and conflict situation.

### August onward — supply/demand balance plus renewed supply risk

The later episode was more mixed in the source analysis.

- OPEC+ additional production initially pushed prices lower.
- Demand forecasts were revised down in some outlooks.
- New supply risks then reintroduced an upward premium.
- WTI moved from roughly **$80.34 in early August to $100.05 by mid-September** in the source snapshot.

The source therefore described this regime as a tug-of-war between **incremental supply and softer demand expectations on one side, and renewed supply risk on the other**.

## How the two regimes transmitted into companies

| Company | Industry | March episode | Since August | Year to date |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Occidental (OXY) | Energy production | +37.0% | +10.8% | +45.0% |
| ExxonMobil (XOM) | Integrated energy | +12.1% | +7.0% | +35.3% |
| Chevron (CVX) | Integrated energy | +11.6% | +10.8% | +37.3% |
| Dow (DOW) | Chemicals | +31.8% | -2.9% | +19.6% |
| Delta (DAL) | Airlines | -7.4% | -12.8% | +15.7% |
| United Airlines (UAL) | Airlines | -21.7% | -14.5% | -2.8% |

### Direct beneficiaries

Energy producers and integrated oil companies benefited most directly from higher realized oil prices in the source analysis. OXY showed the strongest response in the March episode.

### Direct cost pressure

Airlines were hurt in both regimes as higher oil prices raised jet-fuel costs. UAL and DAL both weakened across the two periods in the source snapshot.

### Regime-dependent effects

Chemicals did not behave consistently across the two episodes. DOW rallied sharply in the March period but weakened after August, illustrating why feedstock costs, pricing power, and end-demand conditions need to be separated rather than treating “oil up” as one fixed sector signal.

## How to use the result

The useful conclusion is not simply “buy energy and sell airlines when oil rises.”

First determine **why** oil is rising. Then ask how that driver affects selling prices, input costs, demand, and margins for each industry.

To update the analysis, monitor variables such as:

- actual supply disruption and shipping conditions;
- OPEC+ production policy;
- demand forecasts;
- refining margins and fuel costs;
- whether geopolitical risk premia are expanding or fading.

## Caveats

- Regime returns use representative dates and also contain company-specific earnings, rates, and idiosyncratic news.
- The qualitative regime labels reflect the QUICK-provided source analysis at the time.

This is a cross-asset framework for separating apparently similar commodity rallies by cause, then tracing those differences into sector and company sensitivity.
